Move, then a clear rule, then affection

SubX editorial Example partner post Human + AI

Sponsored by Dog Academy

Many homes offer affection first, when a dog is already spinning, and then wonder why the spinning continues. One optional session plan is a short walk, practice of one familiar cue, and calm affection if the dog welcomes it. This is a session plan for ordinary days. It is not a plan for barking, fear, or aggression. If you are worried about safety or health, stop and call a qualified trainer or a veterinarian.

Set a timer for ten minutes so the session has an end. You need a leash or a long line in a safe yard, one rule you already use, and a calm place to finish. The rule should be something small you can see, such as sitting before the door opens, or walking to a mat. Do not add a second rule today.

Step one, move. Spend about five minutes letting the dog walk, sniff, or trot, with you setting a steady pace. You are spending energy on purpose, not wearing the dog out as a fix. If the dog pulls hard, turn and walk the other way rather than arguing. Keep your voice low. If the dog cannot be near you without biting the leash, end the session and get help. Do not turn this step into a contest.

Step two, one rule. After the movement, ask for the single rule once. Wait. If you get it, mark it with a quiet word you already use, such as yes. If you do not get it, show the dog what you meant one time, then ask once more. Two tries is enough. A long lecture teaches nothing useful and makes you louder.

Step three, affection. Only after the rule, and only if the dog is not jumping into your face, offer a slow pet or a quiet minute of company. If the dog escalates the moment you reach down, put your hands away and end. Affection is the last step, not a reward you sprinkle on chaos. You can also skip affection and just end. Ending cleanly is allowed.

Repeat on another day with the same rule before you change anything. Keep a note of the order you used, not a grade of the dog. Some days the movement is the whole session. That is still practice. None of this replaces a plan from a qualified trainer when a dog is dangerous, and none of it replaces a veterinarian when a dog might be hurt or ill.
