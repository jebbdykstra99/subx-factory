Stay quiet at the door and when you greet

Dog Academy staff Human-made

Sponsored by Dog Academy

Inspired by widely shared training tips from Cesar Millan.

Official site of Cesar Millan

Not affiliated with or endorsed by Cesar Millan.

The front door is where a lot of noise starts. A person arrives, the dog launches, everyone talks at once, and the greeting becomes a sport. This practice slows the first seconds of an arrival. It does not promise a silent dog, and it does not fix fear or guarding. If your dog bites, pins, or cannot be safely held when someone comes in, do not practice with real guests. Call a qualified trainer, and see a veterinarian if the behavior might be tied to pain or illness.

You need one helper, a leash, and a door you can close. The helper can be a person who lives with you. Tell the helper the whole plan before anyone knocks, so nobody improvises a party.

Step one. Put the dog on leash. Stand to the side of the door, not in the swing of it. Ask the helper to knock once and then wait. You do not open the door yet. If the dog barks, you wait too. You are not correcting the bark with a speech. You are letting the knock be a cue to pause. When there is a small break in the noise, or after twenty seconds, go to step two anyway so the drill has a shape.

Step two. Open the door a few inches. The helper does not reach for the dog and does not use a high happy voice. They stand still and look at you, not at the dog. If the dog stays low enough that you can hold the leash without being towed, open the door the rest of the way and let the helper step in and stop. If the dog is too much, close the door and reset. One reset is the lesson. Do not turn it into ten.

Step three. The greeting, if you do one, is brief and low. A hand offered low, a quiet hello, then the helper walks to a chair. No hugging the dog, no face kisses, no excited names shouted from the porch. After one minute, the helper can leave and you can try a second knock if everyone is still calm. Two reps is a full session.

Practice with the same helper on three different days before you invite a new person. New people are a harder version, not the first version. Keep your own voice slow on purpose. This drill only covers ordinary greetings with a dog you can hold. For aggression at the door, fear that looks like panic, or any health concern, stop the rehearsal and ask a qualified trainer or a veterinarian what to do next.
