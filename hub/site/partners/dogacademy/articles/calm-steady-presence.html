Practice a calm, steady presence

Dog Academy staff Human-made

Sponsored by Dog Academy

Inspired by widely shared training tips from Cesar Millan.

Official site of Cesar Millan

Not affiliated with or endorsed by Cesar Millan.

A walk often goes the way the person feels before the leash is even clipped on. This practice is about showing up steady. It is a people skill you can rehearse in the hallway. It is not a treatment, and it is not a promise that a dog will become quiet or easy. If your dog growls, snaps, freezes, or seems sick or sore, stop and speak with a qualified trainer or a veterinarian before you continue.

Pick a time when you are not already late. Leave the leash on a hook. Stand where the dog can see you, and do nothing for one full minute. Drop your shoulders. Breathe out a little longer than you breathe in. Look at a point on the wall, not into the dog's eyes. Do not narrate. Do not cheer. The only job in this minute is to notice whether you are rushing. If you are rushing, stay for another minute. Ending the rush is the first rep.

Step one. When the minute is quiet, pick up the leash and hold it at your side. If the dog jumps, spins, or barks, put the leash back on the hook and return to the quiet minute. You are teaching that the leash shows up with a calm room, not with a loud one. Do not scold. Scolding adds noise. Do not clap either. Clapping adds noise too.

Step two. Walk to the door with the leash in your hand and the dog not yet attached, if that is safe in your home. If the dog must be leashed to be safe, clip it on only after the quiet minute, then walk to the door. Put your hand on the knob. Count to ten in your head. Open the door a small crack and close it. Repeat three times. You are practicing the doorway, not completing an outing.

Step three. If the dog is still manageable, take ten slow steps outside and ten slow steps back in. Keep the leash short enough to gather and loose enough that you are not dragging. Say almost nothing. When you are back inside, remove the leash. Offer a calm touch only if the dog is standing or sitting with a soft body. If the dog is still bouncing, skip the touch and end there.

Do this once a day. Write down the date and whether you kept the quiet minute. Do not score the dog. A session that stops early is still useful if you stayed steady. This drill will not fit every dog and every house. Use it as a warm up, then go back to your ordinary routine. For aggression, panic, or any health worry, ask a qualified trainer or a veterinarian instead of pushing through.
